From: The Scapegoat Unveiled: René Girard and the Secret History of Violence (Suggested Title)
Part II: The Revelation

Chapter 5: The Christian Revelation: A Myth to End All Myths

Introduction: From Dismissal to Discovery

Many of us, particularly in a secularizing West, might recall moments of youthful intellectual pride in dismissing biblical narratives as literal impossibilities. Phrases like "Moses didn't really part the Red Sea" or "Cain and Abel weren't actual historical figures" served as easy justifications for setting aside these ancient texts. For decades, I myself walked this path, relegating the Bible to the realm of outdated fiction. However, encountering the work of René Girard marked a profound turning point. It wasn't about suddenly accepting these stories as literal historical accounts, but about beginning to take them seriously.

Girard offered a lens through which the seemingly archaic story of Cain and Abel, for instance, revealed a startlingly relevant pattern of mimetic rivalry – the kind of envy-fueled conflict that mirrors struggles in our own lives. Whether historically factual or not, these narratives spoke enduring truths about the constants of the human condition. Furthermore, Girard illuminates how deeply Western thought, even in its most secular forms, is steeped in Christian concepts. Assumptions we may take for granted, such as a fundamental concern for victims, are shown to be deeply rooted in the Christian tradition. If Christian ideals like compassion sometimes feel "obvious," it's not a sign of their triviality, but a testament to their immense historical influence.

This chapter delves into Girard's unorthodox yet compelling reading of Jesus Christ and the Christian scriptures. As we explored pagan religion in the previous chapter and will turn to modernity and the potential for apocalypse in the subsequent ones, Christianity stands as the crucial rupture, the bridge connecting our violent pagan past to our complex present and uncertain future. If our modern world seems both eerily similar to and yet distinct from the pagan societies ruled by violence, deceit, and injustice, Girard argues that Christianity is the primary reason for that distinction. For him, Christianity is not merely one religion among many; it is the one true religion precisely because it reveals the hidden mechanisms of violence and scapegoating that underpin false, pagan religions. It shifts history from a cyclical loop of violence and temporary peace into a linear trajectory, demanding we confront the meaning and direction of our collective story. Understanding Girard's interpretation of Christianity is therefore essential not just for theological insight, but for making sense of our present historical moment.

This exploration will proceed in three key stages:

  1. The Hebrew Bible's Distinctive Voice: We will examine why Girard views the stories of the Old Testament as radically different from pagan myths, focusing on their emerging concern for the victim, even while acknowledging their incompletion.
  2. Jesus Christ: The Fullness of Revelation: We will explore Girard's argument for Jesus as the completion of the Hebrew message, the ultimate exposer of the scapegoat mechanism, and why Girard considers him the unique, true manifestation of the divine.
  3. The Anthropology of the Cross: We will translate core Christian doctrines and concepts into Girard's framework of cultural, psychological, and social dynamics, revealing a Christianity grounded in human experience.

Part 1: Echoes of Truth in the Hebrew Bible – A Shift in Perspective

Our first challenge is to differentiate Hebrew Bible narratives from the pagan myths discussed previously. This task is complicated by Girard's own theory of how pagan religions are formed, a process rooted in social chaos and scapegoating:

  1. Mimetic Contagion: Society descends into chaos driven by escalating, copycat rivalries.
  2. The Founding Murder: An individual or group is unconsciously chosen as a scapegoat, blamed for the chaos, and killed or expelled.
  3. Miraculous Peace & Deification: The unanimous violence against the scapegoat brings a sudden, seemingly miraculous peace. The crowd attributes this peace-bringing power to the victim, deifying them.
  4. Myth & Ritual: Myths are created to recount this event (from the persecutors' perspective, justifying the violence), and rituals (prohibitions against renewed chaos, ritualized re-enactments of the violence) emerge to maintain order.

Crucially, Girard asserts that pagan religion is built on a deceit: the victim was innocent, possessing neither the power to cause the chaos nor the power to end it. This was a psychological projection by the unified crowd. Myth masks this deceit because it's told from the perspective of the persecutors, who genuinely believe their actions were justified and the victim's power (good and evil) is real. Revealing the mechanism would shatter the perceived power of the pagan gods.

This framework presents an immediate problem: Doesn't the story of Christ follow this pattern quite closely?

  • Mimetic Contagion: Civil unrest in Jerusalem upon Christ's arrival.
  • Founding Murder: The unjust scapegoating and crucifixion of Jesus.
  • Deification: The Resurrection and subsequent belief in Christ's divinity.
  • Myth & Ritual: The Bible narrative and the institutionalization through the Church with its rituals and prohibitions.

If the form is so similar, how can one be true and the others false? Girard's profound insight lies in a subtle but critical difference: perspective. While pagan myths are told from the persecutors' viewpoint, justifying the violence and masking the victim's innocence, the Bible, starting subtly in the Old Testament and culminating in the Gospels, tells the same type of story but from the perspective of the victim.

By adopting the victim's vantage point, the Bible systematically exposes the lies inherent in the pagan structure:

  • It reveals the victim's innocence.
  • It highlights the crowd's guilt.
  • It denies the victim possesses any sacred power derived from the violence.
  • It shows that the perceived power was merely psychological projection.

Paradoxically, the Bible, for Girard, functions as a religion to end all religions, a myth to end all myths, a founding murder to end all founding murders. It achieves this by laying bare the violent, unjust, and deceitful origins common to all worldly founding events based on scapegoating. Christ understood that analytical reason alone wouldn't dismantle this deeply ingrained cultural practice. What was needed was an equally compelling story—one that fundamentally shifts our moral framework, prompting an automatic identification with the underdog, the victim, thereby allowing us to see through the lies of the scapegoat mechanism.

A helpful metaphor is the relationship between a vaccine and a disease. A vaccine works because of its structural similarity to the original pathogen, containing just enough difference to neutralize it without causing the full illness. The COVID vaccine resembles the COVID virus; that's why it works against COVID and not polio. Similarly, the Bible is a myth vaccine. Its power lies not in being radically different from pagan myths, but in its proximity coupled with a crucial difference (the perspective). Efforts by some modern Christians to distance the Bible entirely from older myths (like the Epic of Gilgamesh, with its shared motifs of immortality trees, deceitful snakes, and great floods) are misguided, according to Girard. The Bible's truth and efficacy stem precisely from engaging with and subverting the mythological structure from within.

Let's examine key Hebrew Bible stories where this intuition of the victim's innocence begins to emerge:

Case Study 1: Cain and Abel (Genesis 4)

  • The Narrative: Cain (farmer, firstborn) and Abel (shepherd, second born) offer sacrifices. God favors Abel's. Out of jealousy, Cain murders Abel. God condemns Cain to wander ("a fugitive and a vagabond"). Fearing retribution, Cain receives a protective mark from God ("whosoever slayeth Cain, vengeance shall be taken on him Sevenfold"). This marks the founding of the Canaanite community.
  • Key Differences from Pagan Myth:
    1. Innocence of the Victim & Injustice of the Act: Unlike Romulus killing Remus (where Remus transgressed by mocking Romulus's walls, providing justification), Abel is depicted as innocent and righteous. Cain's motive is pure envy. God's first words to Cain ("Where is Abel thy brother?") are a moral condemnation, not a pragmatic inquiry. The founding murder is explicitly unjust.
    2. Violence Begets Violence, Not Peace: In pagan myths (Oedipus's expulsion bringing peace to Thebes, Purusha's dismemberment founding Hindu order), the founding violence brings resolution. Here, Cain's immediate reaction is fear of reciprocal violence. God's Mark is a prohibition meant to stop the cycle. However, this legalism fails. Cain's descendant, Lamech, escalates the principle, boasting after killing a man, "If Cain shall be avenged Sevenfold, truly Lamech seventy and sevenfold" (Genesis 4:24). What began as a divine rule to prevent the blood feud within three generations becomes a human war cry inviting it. The scale escalates (7 to 77), and the authority shifts from God (preventing violence) to man (seeking violence).
  • The Message: The victim (Abel) is innocent, the murder unjust, and cultures born from such violence are doomed to cyclical conflict, inadequately contained by legal barriers.

Case Study 2: Joseph (Genesis 37-50)

  • The Narrative: Joseph, the favored 11th son, interprets dreams suggesting his brothers' subservience. Envied and resented, his brothers sell him into slavery in Egypt. Falsely accused of attempted adultery by his master's wife (after resisting her advances), he is imprisoned. His dream interpretation skills eventually lead him to become Pharaoh's right-hand man. During a famine, his brothers come seeking aid (not recognizing him). Joseph tests their character, threatening to enslave the youngest, Benjamin. Judah offers himself in Benjamin's place. Seeing their change, Joseph reveals himself, forgives them, and the family reunites.
  • Key Differences from Pagan Myth:
    1. Unjust Expulsions: Joseph exhibits hubris (annoying his brothers), which in pagan myths might justify expulsion (like Oedipus's pride). Yet, his sale into slavery is portrayed as entirely unjust. His innocence, not his minor flaws, is emphasized. This is the victim's perspective. His second expulsion (imprisonment for alleged adultery/incestuous transgression against a father-figure's wife, a common scapegoat charge like Oedipus's) is also shown to be based on lies.
    2. Forgiveness and Humanization, Not Sacrifice and Deification: The climax isn't another expulsion or sacrifice, but Judah's self-sacrifice offer and Joseph's forgiveness. The result isn't the creation of a sacred, more-than-human figure. Instead, the characters are humanized. The story culminates in profound human emotion:

      "And he fell upon his brother Benjamin's neck and wept; and Benjamin wept upon his neck" (Genesis 45:14).

      Like Jesus later, Joseph wept. We get humanity, not sacrality.
  • The Message: Even seemingly 'justified' expulsions by pagan standards are revealed as unjust from the victim's view. The resolution lies in forgiveness and reconciliation, not sacred violence.

The broader narrative of the Jewish people, centered around their identity as victims (e.g., the Exodus from Egypt, told from the Hebrew/scapegoat perspective, not the Egyptian/persecutor perspective), reinforces this theme. While pagan myths either justify violence (Oedipus) or whitewash it (Purusha), the Hebrew Bible insists on showing the violence as unjustified, revealing the victims' innocence, the injustice of their expulsion, and the delusion of the persecuting crowd. The core intuition brewing is captured in Psalm 40:6 (or similar verses):

"Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire... burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not required."

The Hebrew God is praised for not wanting sacrifices like pagan gods.

Incompletion of the Hebrew Message

Despite this radical shift, Girard, like many Christian theologians, sees the Hebrew Bible as incomplete:

  • In Content: It doesn't fully renounce violence and sacrifice. God still accepts sacrifices (Cain, Abel), demands a near-sacrifice (Isaac), uses violence (Red Sea, plagues, flood), and requires ritual offerings.
  • In Form: It remains tied to legalism – prohibitions and rituals. This is the same legalism that failed to contain Cain's lineage's violence and, tragically, would contribute to Christ's death through figures like the Pharisees and scribes, the Jewish legal authorities.

This sets the stage for Jesus Christ, who, for Girard, will complete the Hebrew revelation in both content and form.

Part 2: Jesus Christ – The Fullness of Revelation and the One True God

Jesus represents the culmination of the Hebrew Bible's tentative steps towards revealing the victim's innocence and rejecting sacred violence. He fulfills its message by radicalizing its content and transcending its legalistic form.

Completion Through the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7)

This pivotal teaching articulates the core of Christian ethics, directly addressing the Hebrew Bible's limitations:

  • Beyond Tit-for-Tat (Content):

    "Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also... Love your enemies, bless them that curse you..." (Matthew 5:38-39, 44).

    Jewish law (Leviticus) demanded proportional retribution, refraining from violent initiative but permitting violent response. Jesus recognizes this isn't enough. In mimetic rivalries, each party feels wronged, perceiving their actions as defensive. Like nations claiming they won't strike first with nuclear weapons, this offers little comfort, as misinterpretation can easily trigger catastrophe. The only way out of the cycle is unilateral renunciation of violence, even when wronged. While the Old Testament delegitimized some violence, Jesus demands rejecting it entirely.
  • Beyond Legalism (Form):

    "Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill... But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment..." (Matthew 5:21-22).

    Jesus moves beyond external actions to internal states – anger, contempt. Legal rules are insufficient because human nature, corrupted by mimetic desire (Girard's "original sin"), can twist any system. The Mark of Cain, intended by God to stop violence, was co-opted by Lamech into a logic for violence. Similarly, modern well-intentioned laws, like minimum wage increases championed to help labor and small businesses, can be weaponized by powerful entities like Amazon, crushing the very groups the law aimed to protect. Legalism inevitably fails because humans find ways to corrupt it. Jesus offers the "spirit" over the "letter," demanding an internal transformation that rules alone cannot achieve.

The Crucifixion: The Founding Murder to End All Murders

The crucifixion is the ultimate event where the Hebrew intuition of the victim's innocence reaches its zenith. It's the myth that exposes all other myths, the religious event designed to end religion (in its pagan, scapegoating sense).

Structural Parallels to Pagan Scapegoating (The Vaccine's Similarity):

  1. Mimetic Contagion: The Jerusalem crowd rapidly switches from acclamation ("Hosanna!") to condemnation ("Crucify him!"), typical of contagious mob frenzy (cf. Ajax, French Revolution).
  2. Founding Murder: Unanimous condemnation – Jewish authorities, Roman political power (Pilate), common criminals (thieves crucified alongside), and even Jesus's own disciples (Peter's denials) converge against him.
  3. Reconciliation through Exclusion: Pilate (Roman) and Herod (Jewish ruler), previously enemies, find common ground in opposing Christ.
  4. Divinization/Institutionalization: Resurrection, the Bible narrative, the Church, rituals, etc.

If the story ended here, it would be just another pagan myth for Girard. But the details, seen through the victim's perspective, reveal its subversive power.

The Crucial Differences (The Vaccine's Efficacy):

The narrative relentlessly focuses on exposing the mechanism and affirming Christ's unique status through three key qualities:

  1. Innocence:
    • Origin: Begins with the Virgin Birth – clean, non-violent, contrasting sharply with the rape-like origins of many pagan gods (e.g., Dionysus).
    • Judgment: Pontius Pilate, the very judge sentencing him, declares,

      "I find no fault in this man" (Luke 23:4).

      Pilate's wife also pleads for his release. His condemnation comes only under mob pressure. There's no ambiguity: the victim is innocent, the mob guilty.
    • Perfection Amidst Chaos: Christ maintains perfect innocence even within the intense mimetic contagion, suggesting a nature not subject to ordinary human failings.
    • Impact: This unambiguous innocence, propagated by Christianity, becomes a dominant moral lens. We learn to look for persecution, side with victims, and distrust mob unanimity. This awareness slowly dismantles the scapegoat mechanism, which requires belief in the victim's guilt for catharsis and peace. Without catharsis, peace fails; without peace, the sacred facade crumbles. Christ tears down the pillars of deceitful worldly power.
  2. Truth:
    • Understanding the Mechanism: Jesus wasn't just a victim; he understood victimization. Girard interprets parables and sayings as revealing this awareness. The "Curse against the Pharisees" is key:

      "That the blood of all the prophets, which was shed from the foundation of the world, may be required of this generation; From the blood of Abel unto the blood of Zechariah..." (Luke 11:50-51).

      "Foundation of the world" (Greek katabolēs kosmou) implies not creation, but the founding of worldly order passing from chaos to order – precisely the scapegoat mechanism based on victims' blood (Abel to Zechariah representing all victims).
    • Poetic Victory: Jesus was victimized because he exposed victimization. His murder became the ultimate proof of his claims. He trapped the mechanism: ignore him, and his message spreads; kill him (as they did), and his death validates and amplifies his message about scapegoating. His silencing became his loudest proclamation.
    • Divinity: Accessing and articulating this profound truth about human society while engulfed in the irrational frenzy of the scapegoat mechanism signifies, for Girard, a superhuman, divine capacity.
  3. Love:
    • The Only Alternative: Unconditional love ("Love your enemies...") is presented as the sole escape from the cycle of violence, replacing the deceitful 'solution' of scapegoating. It's incredibly difficult, requiring universal adoption to prevent the non-violent from becoming victims themselves.
    • Embodied on the Cross: At the peak of suffering and betrayal, Christ embodies his teaching:

      "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34).

    • Divinity: Demonstrating such profound love in the most hostile environment conceivable is, for Girard, perhaps the strongest sign of divinity. It's easy to preach love in a peaceful monastery, far harder amidst wrongful persecution and pain. Christ achieved the latter.

In summary, the Crucifixion, through Christ's unique Innocence, Truth, and Love, exposed the scapegoat mechanism, mortally wounding it. His death confirmed the truth he preached, and his love offered the only genuine path away from violence. The ability to embody these qualities perfectly, especially under such duress, is what convinces Girard of Christ's status as the one true God, distinct from the false, power-based pagan deities.

Part 3: The Anthropology of the Cross – Translating Faith into Human Terms

Girard undertakes a remarkable project: translating core Christian concepts, often understood in purely theological or metaphysical terms, into the tangible language of anthropology, psychology, and sociology. He grounds the 'otherworldly' in 'this world'. We previously touched upon:

  • Original Sin: Girard identifies this with metaphysical desire – the restless, mimetic, prideful urge to be like the other, the unsatisfiable desire for ontological fullness.
  • Fallenness / Worldly Evil: These are the inevitable psychological and social pathologies arising from mimetic desire: fetishization, alienation, bipolar swings between rivalry and adoration, masochism, sadism, oppression, systemic inequality.

Let's extend this anthropological dictionary:

Satan:

For Girard, Satan is not just a single entity but the name for the entire mimetic process seen as a whole. He is the frenzy leading to chaos (disorder) AND the violent, deceitful mechanism that establishes false order through scapegoating.

“He is the source not merely of rivalry and disorder but of all the forms of lying order inside which humanity lives. Human beings are Sons of Satan because they are sons of this murder [the founding murder].”

  • Satan as Worldly Power: If the scapegoat mechanism founds all traditional cultures and power structures, then Satan isn't lurking in the shadows trying to overthrow order; he is the worldly order, the "prince of this world." All false pagan gods, grounded in scapegoating, are satanic. Institutions like law, insofar as they rely on violence and mask foundational injustices, are manifestations of Satan. Even the peace derived from scapegoating is satanic because it's built on an innocent victim's suffering. By participating in and benefiting from such structures, Girard suggests, we all partake in Satan's reign.
  • Explicit Satanism: While all worldly power is tainted, ideologies like Nazism that overtly reject Christian concern for victims and embrace scapegoating are explicitly satanic for Girard.

Christian Revelation:

This is precisely the exposure and defeat of the scapegoat mechanism (Satan) through the Crucifixion. Christ wounds and begins to expel Satan from the world by revealing his methods. The core prescription stemming from this revelation isn't primarily ritual or dogma, but unwavering, unconditional love for everyone, as exemplified by Christ. This love is the only force capable of truly overcoming Satan (the mimetic process).

Antichrist:

This figure is distinct from Satan. Satan represents direct opposition to Christ's message (e.g., reactionary, overt persecution). The Antichrist is more subtle, operating by competing with and subverting Christ from within, often by mimicking him.

  • Co-opting Victim Concern: Girard identifies the Antichrist with modern movements, particularly "progressive totalitarianism," that claim the mantle of victim advocacy but use it as a pretext for new forms of persecution. While Nazism (Satan) failed to eradicate the concern for victims, the Antichrist appropriates it, radicalizes it, and "paganizes" it. Think of the horrors committed in the Soviet Union in the name of protecting the proletariat. This "other totalitarianism" outflanks Christianity "on the left wing," denouncing Christian concern as hypocritical while carrying out its own oppressive crusades under the banner of liberation. It appears Christ-like but leads astray. Today, persecution is often deemed acceptable only when framed as protecting some other victim group.

“[The Antichrist is] the attempt by Nietzsche and Hitler to make humankind forget the concern for victims has ended in a failure... but it is not Christianity that profits... it is rather what I think must be called the other totalitarianism... the one with the greatest future... it does not oppose judeo-christian aspirations but claims them as its own... The other totalitarianism does not openly oppose Christianity but outflanks it on the left wing... the most powerful anti-christian movement is the one that takes over and radicalizes the concern for victim in order to paganize it... [It] presents itself as the Liberator of humanity... they denounce the Christian concern for victims as hypocritical...” (Paraphrased from Girard's likely meaning in the transcript)

Kingdom of God:

This is not solely an afterlife concept for Girard. It signifies a state achievable in this world defined by "the complete and definitive elimination of every form of vengeance... reprisal... [and] violence" between humans. It's the social realization of universal, unconditional love and non-violence, made possible by Christ's revelation and prescription. It requires everyone's participation, as partial adherence simply creates new victims (like Christ himself).

“The significance of the kingdom of God is completely clear it is always a matter of bringing together the warring Brothers of putting an end to the memetic crisis by a universal renunciation of violence the kingdom of God means the complete and definitive elimination of every form of Vengeance in every form of reprisal in relation between men.”

Apocalypse:

This is the likely outcome if humanity fails to establish the Kingdom of God. With the scapegoat mechanism exposed and weakened, its ability to temporarily contain violence through deceit is diminished. Without the old 'safety valve' and without a universal embrace of Christ's non-violent love, humanity is susceptible to unrestrained, global mimetic contagion. The violence will be human-caused, not divine punishment. Girard explicitly links this to modern destructive capabilities, using images like nuclear explosions. Apocalypse is the logical consequence of possessing immense power without the moral framework (love, non-violence) to control our mimetic nature.

The Translated Christian Story:

Let's re-tell the traditional Christian narrative using Girard's anthropological dictionary:

  1. Original Story: Imitating Satan, we developed Original Sin, responsible for worldly evil.
    Girardian Translation: Driven by inherent mimetic/metaphysical desire (the urge to be like others), humans generate psychological and social pathologies (rivalry, alienation, inequality).
  2. Original Story: This Original Sin led us to worship Satan in the guise of false idols.
    Girardian Translation: Metaphysical desire inevitably leads to escalating conflict (mimetic contagion), resolved only through the violent, deceitful scapegoat mechanism, which expels innocent victims and creates arbitrary pagan gods/sacred orders (forms of Satanic power).
  3. Original Story: Christ exposed Satan and gave us the possibility of freedom.
    Girardian Translation: The Crucifixion, revealing the victim's innocence, exposed the violence and lies of the scapegoat mechanism (Satan), giving humanity the knowledge and agency to potentially build societies not based on hidden violence.
  4. Original Story: Satan and the Antichrist tempt us to this day.
    Girardian Translation: In modernity, overt persecution continues (Satanic, e.g., far-right ideologies), while more subtly, movements persecute victims in the name of ending persecution (Antichristic, e.g., hypocritical elements of far-left ideologies).
  5. Original Story: The Christian Revelation engenders either the Kingdom of God or violent Apocalypse.
    Girardian Translation: With the scapegoat mechanism weakened, humanity stands at a crossroads: embrace universal recognition of shared humanity and renounce violence collectively (Kingdom of God), or, failing that, succumb to unchecked global mimetic violence now that the old restraints are gone (Apocalypse).

This translation renders the Christian narrative intelligible through human psychology and social dynamics, without necessarily invoking direct metaphysical intervention for its core mechanisms.

Part 4: Girard's Unorthodox Christianity

This powerful anthropological translation, while illuminating, results in a distinctly unorthodox interpretation of Christianity, challenging several traditional views:

1. Rejection of Sacrificial Atonement:

Girard vehemently rejects the common reading that God the Father demanded Christ's death as a sacrifice to redeem humanity's sins.

  • Why: This makes God resemble a pagan deity, requiring blood for appeasement (like Aztec gods). It misses the point: the mob, driven by universal human scapegoating tendencies, killed Christ. Attributing agency to God absolves humanity of responsibility for the murder, turning it into a divine transaction rather than a revelation of human sinfulness we must confront.

    • "To interpret it as sacrifice absolves man the responsibility of murder it sugarcoats the crucifixion as a result of a Divine pact rather than the misguided Deeds of man which we must introspect and correct."

  • Girard's View: God did not send the mob; He allowed human violence to expose itself. Christ's death wasn't a substitutionary payment for sin, but an exposure of sin. It gives us the possibility of redemption by understanding our own violence, not automatic redemption. If there's sacrifice, it's God/Christ sacrificing self for others (like a firefighter), not demanding others be sacrificed.

2. Redefinition of Divinity:

Traditional arguments for Christ's divinity often focus on miracles (water to wine, walking on water) and the Resurrection.

  • Girard's Objection: Pagan gods also perform miracles and have resurrection stories. These are signs of power, the hallmark of the pagan sacred. Relying on these makes Christ seem like just another "conjurer of cheap tricks." The 3 days between death and resurrection are key because they *break* the instantaneous link between mob violence and sacred peace found in paganism.
  • Girard's View: Christ's true divinity lies in his perfect morality (Innocence, Truth, Love) maintained in the face of ultimate hostility, and his profound humanism (experiencing suffering, crying out "My God, my God, why hast Thou forsaken me?" Matthew 27:46). His refusal to use miraculous power to save himself on the cross is more indicative of his unique divinity than performing miracles would be. The Crucifixion is a consequence of his (moral) divinity colliding with a violent world, not the cause of it (as it would be for a pagan god deified by the event). He replaces the pagan sacred (Power/Powerless) with the Christian divine (Good/Evil).

    • "Instead of making the crucifixion a cause of Jesus's Divinity it is preferable to see it as a consequence of Christ's Divinity."

3. Humanistic and This-Worldly Focus:

Girard's translation is so effective at explaining Christian phenomena in psychological and social terms that it de-emphasizes the metaphysical, otherworldly aspects central to much Christian tradition.

  • Emphasis: Apocalypse is human-caused; Satan is the mimetic process; Christ's defining traits are perfected human virtues; eternal life is rarely discussed; Christ's primary message becomes achieving this-worldly peace rather than securing otherworldly salvation.
  • Lack: Compared to Augustine, Aquinas, Dante, or Milton, Girard offers little on the ontology of heaven, the nature of the Trinity, Purgatory, angelic realms, or divine judgment of souls. His quote about being "liberated... with no father in the sky to punish us" (meaning a God who constantly intervenes with punishments, not denying God's existence) captures this humanistic, non-interventionist feel. For some, this may feel like an "atheistic Christianity," where God's direct action is minimized.

    • "So now we are liberated we know that we are by ourselves with no father in the sky to punish us and interfere with our paltry business Divine punishment is demystified by the gospels it's only place nowadays is in the Mythic imagination."

    "Christ's Triumph take place in a Beyond of which we can describe neither the time nor place." (Girard's rare comment on the beyond)

4. Critique of Historical/Institutional Christianity:

If the core Christian message is anti-violence, anti-power, and anti-scapegoating, then any worldly institution built upon it risks distorting that message.

  • The Oxymoron: "Christian power" is almost a contradiction in terms for Girard. True Christianity undermines worldly power structures.

    • "The gospels can serve as a foundation for a new culture similar to all the previous cultures only as a result of a certain Distortion of the original message."

  • Distortion: Historical Christianity, insofar as it became a religion with worldly power, founding cultures, engaging in persecution (of Jews, Muslims, etc.), and developing complex rituals and prohibitions like pagan religions, represents a perversion or "distortion" of Christ's original message. The deification of Christ sometimes mimicked pagan practices, masking the human responsibility revealed in the Gospels.
  • True Christianity: For Girard, being truly Christian means aspiring to unconditional love and non-violence above all else – more fundamental than specific rituals, dogmas, or even belief in the Resurrection or the Father, although these might be supports.

5. The Apocalyptic Paradox:

The ultimate unorthodoxy is that the "good news" itself – the very revelation of truth, love, and the victim's innocence – is inherently destabilizing.

  • Tearing Down Foundations: If human societies historically relied on the violence and deceit of the scapegoat mechanism for peace, then exposing that mechanism (Christ's work) removes the foundation without guaranteeing the alternative (universal love) will take its place.
  • Christ's Sword: Truth and Love, while ultimately good, bring chaos to a world built on lies and violence. This echoes Christ's own words:

    "Think not that I am come to send peace on earth: I came not to send peace, but a sword" (Matthew 10:34).

    Satan brought (false) worldly peace; Christ brings (potentially chaotic) truth.

Conclusion: The Sword's Edge

René Girard presents a challenging, deeply insightful, yet profoundly unorthodox reading of Christianity. He positions it not merely as a faith tradition, but as the pivotal event in human history that reveals the hidden violent structures underlying all human culture. By shifting the narrative perspective to the victim, the Bible, culminating in Christ, offers both an exposé of our deepest anthropological flaws and a radical prescription for healing – unconditional love.

However, this revelation comes at a cost. By weakening the old mechanisms of societal cohesion, however unjust they were, the Christian message unleashes forces it cannot easily control. It places humanity on a precipice, faced with the stark choice between embracing the difficult path towards a global Kingdom of God built on non-violence and mutual recognition, or succumbing to an Apocalypse born of our own unchecked mimetic conflicts. The sword Christ brought has left a deep gash in history, and the following chapters will explore the consequences of that wound as we navigate modernity and confront our potential future.
